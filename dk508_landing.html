Why this existsA library catalog is a historical instrument too

A national library's subject-cataloguing decisions — which place a book is "about," which period it covers, which places share a shelf — are themselves a kind of historical record: not of events, but of how those events have been written about, cross-referenced, and collected over more than a century of cataloguing.

We built this to foster deeper thinking about Ukrainian history and the relationships woven through it — not to replace historians, but to make a large, ordinarily invisible dataset visible and explorable. Every chart here is a starting point for a question, not a final answer: why does this city keep showing up as a bridge between clusters? why does this period suddenly have so much more written about it? why is this book, about a small town in western Ukraine, published in Canada? The data rewards exactly that kind of curiosity, and every marker, band, and bubble links back to the real catalog record so you can go find out.

About the data7,093 catalog records, one classification range

DK508 is the Library of Congress classification range for the history of Ukraine. Every record here comes from bulk bibliographic data for that range: a title, its subject headings (Library of Congress Subject Headings, LCSH), its place and year of publication, its language, and its call number. Nothing here is hand-curated content — it's what several generations of catalogers at the Library of Congress recorded about each book.

Two different kinds of "where" come out of that metadata, and the two visualizations are built around the difference between them:

  • Aboutness — the place(s) a subject heading names, often with a date range attached (e.g. "Kharkiv (Ukraine) — History — 1941-1945"). This is what a record is about, independent of when or where it was published.
  • Publication — where and when the physical book itself was printed. A book published in Toronto in 1962 can be entirely about a village in Volyn in the 1930s.

The geography isn't only UkraineSmall cities, whole oblasts, and everywhere Ukraine's history touches

The natural assumption is that a "Ukraine — History" collection is geographically about Ukraine, full stop. The subject data says otherwise, and that's the most interesting thing the map shows.

At the local end, the collection is genuinely granular: individual cities and towns (Lʹviv, Kharkiv, Berdychiv, Uzhhorod...), and aggregate administrative regions — oblasti and historic regions alike (Ivano-Frankivsʹka oblastʹ, Chernihivsʹka oblastʹ, Boikivshchyna, Podillia). But a meaningful share of subject headings name places entirely outside Ukraine's modern borders, and for good reason: a book about Russo-Ukrainian relations is about both Ukraine and Russia; a book about the Ukrainian diaspora is about Ukraine and Canada, or Ukraine and Argentina; a book about the interwar Polish administration of Galicia is about both.

That last category matters too: not every place on the map still exists. Ancient Greek colonies on the Black Sea coast, the Crimean Khanate, the Zaporozhian Sich, the Soviet Union itself — all of these are subjects the catalog treats as real places with real date ranges, even though none of them appear on a current political map. The map tool includes them (togglable) rather than quietly cutting the collection off at whatever counts as "modern."

RelationshipsPlaces don't just co-occur — they form a structure

Any two places named in the same record's subject headings are treated as connected. Do that across all 7,093 records and a network falls out of the data on its own — nobody drew it by hand.

Two ideas from network analysis do most of the explanatory work in that structure:

Communities

Places that co-occur with each other more than they co-occur with the rest of the network cluster into communities — and in this data, those communities read as regional patterns: western oblasts cluster together, diaspora countries cluster with the regions that produced their emigrants, Crimea and the Donbas cluster with Russia-oriented material. The clustering isn't imposed by us; it's a direct readout of which places this specific bibliography keeps mentioning side by side.

Centrality & structural bridges

Betweenness centrality measures how often a place sits on the shortest path between two other places in the network — in plain terms, how much it acts as a bridge between parts of the collection that otherwise wouldn't connect. A place can have a high bridge score without being especially large, if it's the one thing tying two otherwise-separate clusters together.

Example, from the live data

Donetsʹk oblastʹ consistently ranks as one of the network's strongest structural bridges — not because it dominates any one community, but because of its dual role: a Soviet-era industrial region with sister-city and diplomatic connections that reach into the Cold War-era "Soviet Union" cluster, while simultaneously appearing in present-day war-coverage records that connect it to Western and diaspora material. Filter the network to Russian-language records only, and the bridges shift toward Crimea and the Donbas; filter to Ukrainian-language records, and they shift toward western oblasts and diaspora countries like Canada. Same collection, two different skeletons — depending on which language wrote about it.

Both of these are live in the Map & Network Explorer: pin any place to see its coreness, community, and betweenness rank against the rest of the network, and filter by language to watch the structure reshape itself.

The Russian- and Polish-language presence isn't incidental — it traces the same centuries of imperial rule and cultural contact the Influence River chart plots directly: the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Russian Empire, and the Soviet Union all produced (and required) scholarship on Ukrainian territory in their own languages. English's substantial share reflects the postwar Ukrainian-American and Ukrainian-Canadian diaspora's academic output, as much as it reflects Anglophone scholarly interest. Filtering the map by language doesn't just shrink the dataset — it changes which places and relationships surface at all.

History runs deeper than statehoodIndependence was 1991 — the catalog's coverage isn't

Ukraine has been a formally independent, internationally recognized state since 1991. The catalog's own idea of "Ukrainian history" doesn't start there, or anywhere close to there — its earliest documented subject matter runs back roughly 2,500 years, to Greek colonies on the Black Sea coast.

What this adds to historical understandingThree things this view does that reading the catalog record-by-record can't

This isn't a substitute for reading the books, and it doesn't settle historical arguments on its own. What it's good for is noticing things a straightforward read of 7,093 individual records would never surface, and turning them into questions worth chasing into the actual titles.

Emphasis becomes visible

A single catalog record can't tell you whether it's typical or an outlier for its period — only the shape of many records together can. On the Influence River, Poland's mention-years step from the mid-teens to 33 right at 1918, and Russia's step from 14 to 25 at 1917 — precisely the Ukrainian–Polish War and the Russian Revolution. No reader browsing headings one at a time would notice that step; seen as a shape, it's immediate. That's a claim about how this history has been catalogued and written about, not a verdict on what happened — but it's real evidence of emphasis, and exactly the kind of thing worth opening the actual records behind it to understand further.

Documentation has its own timeline, separate from events

The Map & Network Explorer keeps two timelines deliberately separate: what a record is about versus when it was published. A retrospective history written decades after the events it covers looks identical to a contemporaneous account if you only track one date — conflate the two and you lose the ability to ask "when did people start writing about this," which is its own historical question, distinct from "when did this happen." Splitting them turns the catalog into a rough instrument for watching scholarly and public attention shift over time, not just the events themselves.

Silence isn't proof of absence

The Influence River goes completely flat for Russia, Poland, and every other outside power before 1501, and the "Other powers" band goes quiet twice more, in 1743–1799 and 1801–1866. It would be easy to read a flat line as "nothing happened here" — this view deliberately doesn't let that reading go unchallenged. A gap might mean the catalog genuinely holds nothing describing that period, or it might mean cataloguing practice at the time simply didn't apply this kind of dated heading as often, regardless of what was actually going on historically. The tool can't tell those two apart for you, and doesn't try to — surfacing the question at all is a more careful way to read an archive than assuming its coverage is even. The Map Explorer's structural bridges do something similar for geography: a place like the Soviet Union or Poland scoring high on betweenness is, in a rough sense, a fingerprint of exactly how entangled Ukraine's administrative and documented history has been with its neighbors.

Why this beats reading headings or call numbers directly

Nothing in these tools is information a subject heading or an LCC call number doesn't already contain — every mention-year, every network edge, every place on the map traces back to text a cataloger actually wrote. What changes is what becomes possible to see at the scale of 7,093 records, not one.

A call number puts a book in exactly one place. A number like DK508.833 .L976 encodes this collection's own internal topic/region hierarchy and sorts a book to one fixed shelf position within it — useful for browsing physically-adjacent books, but a book only lives at that one spot. The same record can be about a place, a time period, and a relationship to another power all at once, and a shelf position can't hold three dimensions simultaneously the way a filterable map, timeline, and network graph can — the map/river/network views just let the same 7,093 records get grouped by whichever of those axes is the interesting one right now, instead of the one axis a call number commits to permanently.

A subject heading, read one record at a time in a catalog search, is a list — and a list doesn't add. Noticing that Poland's headings step from 14–15 to 33 right at 1918 means literally tallying hundreds of individual date-subdivided headings by year; no one does that by eye across thousands of records, so that pattern is functionally invisible to a person paging through search results, even though every single heading behind it was sitting right there in the catalog the whole time. The chart isn't adding information the headings lack — it's doing the arithmetic a human browsing them realistically can't, and showing the result as a shape instead of a spreadsheet.

Cross-record relationships fare even worse under plain browsing: two records that share "Kharkiv" and "Poland" as subjects don't visibly connect to each other anywhere in a catalog interface. You'd have to search each heading separately and manually cross-reference hundreds of result lists to notice they cluster — which is exactly the arithmetic the network graph performs for the whole collection at once, turning an invisible pattern spread across thousands of separate search results into a handful of communities and bridge nodes you can see and click through in seconds.

All of this reads the catalog's own cataloguing choices, not history directly — see Being honest about the data for what that proxy can and can't support.

Every bubble is a bookshelfNothing here is a dead end

Aggregate patterns are only useful if you can get back to the real evidence behind them. Every part of the Map & Network Explorer is click-through: pin a place to see the actual titles behind it (sortable by year or alphabetically, linking out to each record's permanent page on loc.gov), search by keyword across the whole collection regardless of the active filters, or hover any point on the Influence River to read real example titles from that decade.

If a pattern looks surprising — a sudden plateau, an unexpected bridge, a diaspora country you didn't expect — the next click is always "show me the books," not just a bigger number.

Being honest about the dataWhat this is — and isn't — evidence of

1

"Aboutness" is documented association, not a territorial or political claim. A place appearing in a subject heading means a cataloger judged a book to be about that place at that time — nothing about legitimacy, control, or ownership.

2

Not every date comes from the subject heading itself. When a heading has no date subdivision, the pipeline falls back to the book's own publication year as a best guess. That fallback covers roughly two-thirds of individual heading instances, but those rows are short (usually a single year), so they contribute far less to any long-run pattern than the smaller share of headings with real, explicit date ranges — still, it's a proxy, not a certainty, and it's the main reason to treat exact year-by-year values as approximate rather than precise.

3

Coverage reflects what the Library of Congress has catalogued, not what was published, or what happened. A gap in the chart is a gap in this collection, not necessarily a gap in history.

4
